# Simon Sinek's Five Rules

Simon Sinek is an author and speaker best known for his work on leadership and organizational culture. During [one of his speeches](https://youtu.be/CZx4DTglHJc), he offered five rules for living and leading well. If you enjoy his work as much as I do, you can support him [here](https://simonsinek.com/).

## 1. Go after the things you want

Two friends finish a weekend race in Central Park, where the sponsor is giving away free bagels. Volunteers stand on one side of a row of picnic tables, and a long line of runners waits on the other. One friend suggests grabbing a bagel. The other declines because the line is too long.

The first friend walks up to the line, leans in between two people, reaches into the box, and pulls out two bagels. Nobody objects. They give up the ability to choose which bagel they get, but they do not have to wait.

The exchange reveals two ways of seeing the world. **Some people see the thing they want. Others see the thing that prevents them from getting it.**

The rule is that **you can go after whatever you want, as long as you do not deny anyone else the chance to go after what they want.** Reaching past a line pushes that boundary, which is the point: the bagel cost no one their place, only the choice of which one to take. You do not have to do things the way everyone else has done them. You can break the rules. You just cannot get in the way of someone else.

## 2. Take accountability

For centuries, childbed fever was one of the most feared risks of giving birth. Women would fall ill within days of delivery, and in some European maternity wards, as many as one in three died.

The 1800s were an era of science and empirical data, and doctors set out to find the cause. They performed autopsies on the women who had died in the morning, then delivered babies in the afternoon.

In 1843, Dr. Oliver Wendell Holmes, father of the Supreme Court Justice of the same name, argued that doctors themselves were carrying the disease from patient to patient. A few years later in Vienna, Dr. Ignaz Semmelweis noticed that the doctors were not washing their hands between autopsies and deliveries. When he required them to wash with a chlorine solution, deaths in his clinic fell dramatically. He was ignored and ridiculed for decades, and handwashing did not become standard until germ theory was accepted later in the century.

The lesson is that **sometimes you are the problem.** You can take all the credit in the world for what you do right, as long as you also take responsibility for what you do wrong. It must be a balanced equation. **You only earn the credit when you also accept the accountability.**

## 3. Take care of each other

The United States Navy SEALs are among the most elite warriors in the world. When a SEAL was asked who makes it through the selection process, he said he could not describe the kind of person who succeeds, but he could describe the kind who does not. Those who try to prove how tough they are, those who lead by delegating everything, and those who have never truly been tested rarely make it through.

Some of those who succeed are skinny and scrawny. Some of them shiver with fear. What they all share is that when they are physically and emotionally spent, with nothing left to give, they still find the energy to help the person next to them.

Being an elite warrior is not about how tough, smart, or fast you are. **It is about how good you are at helping the person to your left and the person to your right.** The world is too dangerous and too difficult to do these things alone.

**Learn to ask for help when you need it and to accept it when it is offered.** It may be the most valuable skill you ever develop. Once you admit that you are stuck, scared, or unsure, you will find that many people around you have always wanted to help. They simply assumed you did not need it because you pretended to have everything under control.

That support only appears, however, if you learn to take care of others first.

## 4. Be the last to speak

Nelson Mandela is widely regarded as a great leader across nations and political perspectives. He was raised by a regent of the Thembu people, and when asked how he learned to lead, he recalled watching the regent preside over tribal meetings. He remembered two things: the elders always sat in a circle, and the regent was always the last to speak.

Most people are told they need to learn to listen. **The more important skill is learning to be the last to speak.** Even capable leaders often walk into a room, state the problem and their own view, and then ask for everyone else's opinion. By then it is too late.

Holding your opinion until everyone has spoken does two things. It gives everyone else the sense that they have been heard and have contributed. It also gives you the benefit of hearing every perspective before you render your own.

The discipline is to keep your opinions to yourself. Do not nod in agreement or shake your head in disagreement. Simply take it all in. The only thing you are allowed to do is ask questions, so that you understand not just what people are saying, but where they are speaking from and why they hold their view. At the end, you get your turn. It sounds easy. It is not.

## 5. You deserve a styrofoam cup

A former Undersecretary of Defense was giving a speech at a large conference. Partway through his prepared remarks, he took a sip of coffee from a styrofoam cup, smiled, and went off script.

The previous year, he had spoken at the same conference while still in office. He was flown in business class, driven to a hotel where he had already been checked in, escorted to the venue through the back entrance, and handed coffee in a beautiful ceramic cup.

This year, no longer the undersecretary, he flew coach, took a taxi, checked himself in, and found his own way backstage. When he asked for coffee, someone pointed to a machine in the corner, and he poured himself a cup into the styrofoam. **The ceramic cup, he said, was never meant for him. It was meant for the position he held.**

The same is true of a former Chief of Staff of the United States Air Force, stepping off a train in Washington, D.C. and dragging his own suitcase down the platform like everyone else. Only months earlier, he flew on private jets with an entourage carrying his luggage.

As you gain fame, fortune, position, and seniority, people will treat you better. They will hold doors, bring you coffee without being asked, call you sir or ma'am, and give you things. None of it is meant for you. It is meant for the position you hold. You can accept the perks and enjoy them, but be grateful for them and remember that they are not for you. **We all deserve a styrofoam cup.**
